## From Segmentation Accuracy to Clinical Robustness: Understanding Observer Variability in Breast MRI-Guided Radiotherapy

**A scientific perspective and research brief**

**Wura Alake**  
PhD Candidate | Nanoscale Organisation and Dynamics Group  
School of Science, Western Sydney University, Australia

*October 2026 | Research perspective and ongoing PhD programme*

---

## Abstract

Deep learning offers considerable potential to improve the efficiency and reproducibility of magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) segmentation for breast radiotherapy. However, conventional approaches to model development and evaluation frequently assume that a single expert annotation represents an objective ground truth. In clinical practice, anatomical boundaries may be interpreted differently by qualified observers, even when established delineation protocols are followed. This raises important questions about how segmentation models learn from variable annotations and how their performance should be evaluated.

Building on foundational research demonstrating promising multi-observer-informed breast MRI segmentation, this perspective considers the limitations of overlap-based evaluation and proposes a research direction centred on the mathematical characterisation of observer variability, geometric robustness and clinical relevance. The central argument is that clinically reliable segmentation requires more than agreement with an individual reference contour: it requires understanding how valid differences in anatomical interpretation influence model behaviour and potentially affect radiotherapy planning.

## 1. Clinical motivation: why do boundaries matter?

Accurate identification of clinical target volumes (CTVs) is fundamental to radiotherapy planning. The objective is to deliver the prescribed radiation dose to the intended treatment region while limiting unnecessary exposure to surrounding healthy tissues.

MRI provides excellent soft-tissue contrast and offers opportunities for improved anatomical visualisation in breast radiotherapy. Its integration with linear accelerators (MRI-Linacs) also enables MRI-guided adaptive radiotherapy, in which treatment plans may be adjusted in response to anatomical changes.

However, CTV delineation remains a challenging and often time-consuming clinical task. Anatomical ambiguity, post-operative changes and differences in boundary interpretation can produce variation between expert observers. In breast radiotherapy, inter-observer variability has been documented across imaging modalities and patient positions (Pogson et al., 2016), while previous multi-institutional research has demonstrated that differences in target delineation can influence treatment planning (Li et al., 2009).

Such variation does not necessarily indicate clinical error. Different observers may produce defensible target boundaries while following established guidance, although individual contours must still be assessed against clinical requirements.

This presents an important challenge for automated segmentation: **how should a model be trained and evaluated when more than one clinically reasonable interpretation of the same anatomy may exist?**

## 2. Research foundation: multi-observer breast MRI segmentation

My foundational Master of Research study investigated the feasibility of deep learning-based segmentation of whole-breast CTVs on post-operative MRI.

The research used a retrospective dataset originating from the work of Pogson et al. (2016). The final model-development cohort comprised 18 patients with T2-weighted breast MRI and contours generated by 11 clinical observers, providing 198 observer-specific segmentation references.

A three-dimensional U-Net was developed using the MONAI framework. Rather than combining all expert annotations into a single consensus mask before training, individual observer contours were retained as separate training labels. This allowed model development and evaluation to incorporate multiple expert-defined anatomical boundaries.

These findings demonstrate promising segmentation overlap and computational efficiency. Importantly, the 198 observer-specific evaluations represent repeated reference comparisons within an 18-patient cohort, rather than 198 independent patients.

The study provides a practical foundation for investigating automated breast CTV segmentation. However, retaining individual annotations during training does not mean the resulting network explicitly learns the full distribution of observer disagreement or provides calibrated estimates of boundary uncertainty.

Furthermore, segmentation accuracy and inference speed do not independently establish readiness for clinical implementation. The study has not demonstrated robustness across independent clinical datasets or established the dosimetric consequences of model-generated boundaries.

These limitations motivate the next stage of research.

## 3. The scientific gap: beyond a single ground truth

The Dice similarity coefficient is widely used to quantify spatial overlap between predicted and reference segmentation masks. Although useful for comparing segmentation outputs, it does not fully characterise boundary behaviour, anatomical uncertainty or clinical consequences.

Two contours may demonstrate high overall overlap while differing in localised regions that are important for target definition. Conversely, a model prediction may exhibit reduced overlap with one observer while remaining compatible with the range of contours produced by other qualified observers.

The *Metrics Reloaded* framework emphasises that evaluation metrics should reflect the underlying scientific and clinical problem, recommending complementary measures where appropriate (Maier-Hein et al., 2024).

A further challenge arises from how reference annotations are represented. Conventional approaches frequently designate a single observer annotation or fused consensus contour as ground truth. Consensus-generation methods, including STAPLE (Warfield et al., 2004), can provide useful reference estimates but may obscure information about the original differences between observers.

The perspectivist approach described by Cabitza et al. (2023) challenges the assumption that disagreement should always be eliminated during the construction of machine-learning reference datasets. Similarly, Wang et al. (2023) demonstrated an approach to learning from multiple annotators that incorporates information about annotator agreement rather than relying exclusively on a single consolidated label.

These developments offer a conceptual foundation for investigating observer variability as potentially informative data.

For breast MRI segmentation, however, an important distinction remains: observer disagreement is not automatically equivalent to acceptable clinical uncertainty. Some differences may reflect genuine anatomical ambiguity, while others may arise from systematic observer bias, inconsistent interpretation or technical limitations.

**The scientific challenge is therefore not simply to preserve disagreement, but to determine which variations are meaningful, how they influence model learning and whether they matter clinically.**

## 4. Towards a mathematical characterisation of observer variability

A central direction of my PhD research is to investigate how observer-dependent anatomical boundaries can be represented mathematically and incorporated into segmentation development and evaluation.

For a given MRI volume, let the collection of observer-specific binary segmentation masks be represented as:

$$
\mathcal{Y}_i = \left\{y_i^{(1)}, \ldots, y_i^{(J_i)}\right\}
$$

where Jᵢ denotes the number of contributing observers and yᵢ⁽ʲ⁾ represents the binary segmentation mask produced by observer j for MRI volume i.

An established starting point for characterising spatial agreement is the voxel-wise observer inclusion frequency:

$$
A_i(v) = \frac{1}{J_i}\sum_{j=1}^{J_i} y_i^{(j)}(v)
$$

where the sum extends over all Jᵢ observers and Aᵢ(v) represents the proportion of observers including voxel v within the target volume.

Values approaching one indicate greater observer agreement on voxel inclusion, while intermediate values identify regions where inclusion varies between observers.

This representation provides an initial description of spatial disagreement. It does not, however, establish the probability that a voxel belongs within the clinically correct target, nor does it independently determine the clinical acceptability of alternative boundaries.

The proposed research will examine whether such representations can be extended through geometric descriptors, surface-distance measures, mathematical constraints and uncertainty-aware approaches.

Of particular interest is whether models can distinguish relatively stable anatomical regions from boundaries exhibiting greater observer-dependent variation, and whether these differences can meaningfully inform segmentation learning.

Rather than assuming that every deviation from an individual observer represents equivalent prediction error, this approach seeks to establish a more informative relationship between expert annotation variability, model behaviour and anatomical reliability.

## 5. Proposed PhD research programme

The overarching aim of this research is to develop and investigate multi-observer-aware deep learning approaches for robust and computationally efficient breast MRI segmentation, with a longer-term application to MRI-Linac adaptive radiotherapy.

Three interconnected research directions will guide this investigation.

### I. Characterising inter-observer variability

The first direction examines how differences between expert delineations influence model training, evaluation and generalisability.

This includes investigating the spatial distribution of disagreement, identifying recurrent regions of anatomical ambiguity and examining the relationship between observer variability and model prediction errors.

The objective is to establish whether measurable patterns of boundary variation can explain aspects of segmentation performance that are not adequately captured by global overlap metrics.

### II. Developing mathematically informed, robust segmentation

The second direction investigates how mathematical descriptions of anatomical and imaging variability can support more robust segmentation methods.

Potential approaches include observer-aware learning objectives, uncertainty representations, geometric constraints and strategies for domain generalisation.

Existing approaches to learning from multiple annotators provide important methodological foundations, but their suitability for breast CTV segmentation and radiotherapy-specific requirements must be established experimentally.

Evaluation will extend beyond conventional Dice scores to include appropriate boundary-sensitive and geometric measures, alongside investigations of robustness to acquisition and anatomical variation.

### III. Evaluating delineation consistency and clinical relevance

The third direction considers how observer variability interacts with established clinical delineation practices.

Rather than replacing existing contouring standards, the research will examine the residual variation that persists when qualified observers apply those standards and investigate whether structured guidance, observer calibration or consistent evaluation practices can improve reproducibility.

An important longer-term objective is to understand whether particular boundary differences influence radiotherapy planning outcomes, including target coverage and exposure of surrounding healthy tissues.

These investigations will require suitable clinical datasets, interdisciplinary collaboration and appropriate clinical validation. They represent proposed research directions rather than completed findings.

## 6. Perspective and anticipated impact

Automated segmentation has the potential to support increasingly efficient MRI-guided radiotherapy workflows. However, computational speed and high agreement with a reference contour are only preliminary indicators of suitability for clinical use.

The broader challenge is to establish how segmentation systems behave when anatomical interpretation is uncertain, clinical observers disagree and imaging conditions vary.

By connecting multi-observer annotation analysis with mathematical characterisation, model robustness and clinically relevant evaluation, this PhD programme aims to contribute towards a more defensible framework for developing breast MRI segmentation methods.

The intended outcome is not to eliminate legitimate clinical judgement or replace established delineation expertise. Rather, it is to develop methods that better account for variability within clinically informed target definitions, support reproducible contouring and provide a stronger foundation for eventual clinical translation.

**Accurate segmentation is a starting point, not the endpoint.**

Clinically robust adaptive radiotherapy AI requires understanding how valid boundary differences influence learning, geometric reliability and treatment planning.
